1Install the app on a computer that's often on.

3Scan the code with your phone.

The phone's camera opens hoi, connects to your computer over your Wi-Fi, encrypted, with nobody in between, and takes your key after you compare four digits on both screens.

before you run a public node

You become responsible.

When you run a public node and invite others, you process their personal data, at least their IP addresses and connection metadata. Under the data protection rules that apply where you, your server and those people are, whoever runs such a node is usually the one responsible for that data (often called the data controller). That is you. We, the developers, are not.

Private use has narrow limits. Many data protection rules exempt purely personal or household use, such as a node only your family and close friends use. A community open to people you do not know personally, even without any commercial purpose, generally does not count as personal use.

What you must do as an operator:

  • Write a join notice. The installer lets you define it, and every user must accept it before your node serves them.
  • Explain in it what your node stores and for how long, including what your server or your hosting provider logs.
  • Keep your server secure and up to date.
  • Respond to requests from users about their data.

Who you are: optional, but you should say. The join notice asks for your name or a pseudonym, a way to reach you, the country your server is in and who hosts it. You can leave any of them empty; the notice then tells people plainly that you did, and that they cannot reach you about their data through it. You should give them: they tell people who answers for their data and how to ask, and many data protection rules expect an operator to be identifiable and reachable. Whatever you fill in is shown to everyone who connects to your node, and travels in its node link, so anyone who has the link can read it.

What the software does for you. The client always shows the standard network and VPN warning. Your join notice is added on top and cannot be skipped by users.

This is not legal advice. Which rules apply depends on where you, your server and the people who connect are, and more than one set may apply at once. If you run a large or organised community, check your obligations with a qualified professional.

The app

desktop installs hoi: it opens as the companion in the tray, with the full app a click away. companion installs the same app and starts it in the tray whenever you log in, so your communities stay reachable while the computer is on.

A home node

home turns a Raspberry Pi, a Mac mini or any computer that stays on into a home node for your phone and browser, and, like every home node, a public relay: it asks your router to forward its ports, carries open communities for anyone who asks, tells whoever connects about the other public nodes it reached, and has a link you can share. No registry needed. private is the same without the public part. Both run in the background, no window.

A public node

public runs a light node with no key of its own that carries open communities and passes on the public nodes it reached. registry adds the public-name API to it, for a community that runs its own names in its own copy of the app; --tls caddy serves its https:// address with a certificate of its own.

For a server

The node setup app (below, with the downloads) installs and manages a public node on a rented server over SSH with a wizard: it checks the server first, asks for your join notice with a live preview, installs the node as a service and hands you its link as text and QR code. Or the app makes the kit: open hoi, then You → A node on a server (straight there). Pick a public node or a registry, keep the random name or roll another, write the join notice, and download one file with the configuration, the notice, the installer and a README; copy it over and run one command, or paste its cloud-init into the server's user data when you order it. Nothing of yours goes in it.

For a server

Running a public node or a registry means answering for other people's data. Read before you run a public node above and tick the box: the server options show up here.

Checks first

Before a node is installed, the installer looks at the computer: system, systemd, rights, the release files, disk, memory, clock, ports, firewall, addresses and DNS. It says ok, warn or BLOCK for each, and stops before changing anything on a BLOCK. install.sh check does only that.

Want to read it first? install.sh and install.ps1 are plain text, and every option is in --help. Prefer clicking? The files are right below.

Browser

nothing to install

hoi runs in a browser tab as a light client. To be honest about it: a browser can only reach others, nobody can reach it, and only while the tab is open. So it syncs through a computer running hoi, yours (paired with one click or one scan) or a member's from your invite, and tells you which ("via lena-laptop"). Your keys and posts still stay in the tab, on your device.

hoi-node

installer or archive

The same core with no window, for a small VPS or a Raspberry Pi in the cupboard. It keeps your communities warm as a home node for your phone and browser and pins their history so newcomers can catch up, and members' browsers can catch up from it over WebRTC with no domain or certificate needed. It is a public relay too: it carries open communities for anyone, passes on the other public nodes it reached, and prints a link anyone can open to find what it carries. The installer sets any of these up as a service; the archives are for doing it by hand. More on running one.
