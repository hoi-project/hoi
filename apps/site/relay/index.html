one switch · no logs

Run a relay. Keep communities reachable.

Keep a public community available when its members are away. Your computer can carry its signed posts and attachments for other devices to catch up. Public carriers can read the public content they hold; every receiving device checks its authorship and rules. Private communities use a separate encrypted relay, with readable content and keys staying on invited devices.

What the switch does

Lets members in

Members' browsers reach it over WebRTC and members' computers over QUIC, from the routes in their invites. Every invite carries the routes of members who were around lately, so anyone whose computer is on can let a newcomer in. Only the communities your computer is in pass through it.

Can't be tampered with

Every connection is encrypted on the way (TLS 1.3 or DTLS), so nobody watching the network reads anything. Every post, vote and decision is signed by its author and checked by every device, yours included: your computer can pass things on, never change them.

Shows what passes, keeps no logs

The app shows how much passed through and when, per connection and minute by minute, and how it was encrypted. Never what a post says, never an address, nothing written down: it starts over when the app does. 0 bytes logged, ever.

Knows its limits

You pick a daily cap; relaying naps until tomorrow when it's reached. Off means only your own devices connect. Blocking single devices comes with the packet relay below.

Is on by default, politely

The desktop app keeps your communities reachable from the start, tells you so on its first run, and can be switched off any time.

Before you turn on relaying

  • 1Your node stores and serves community records. Public records are readable by the peers carrying them. Transport encryption protects the connection; it does not make public content private.
  • 2To pass packets along, your computer sees the IP addresses of the devices it helps, and when and how much they send. The peer code does not write access logs. Public connection routes can be shared in invites or registry announcements; network infrastructure may retain connection metadata.
  • 3You choose a daily cap. When it's reached, relaying naps until tomorrow.
  • 4A signature proves who wrote something, not that it belongs anywhere. Community moderation decides what a community keeps; what your own node carries is up to you.
  • 5You can switch relaying off at any time. Stored community records remain on your device until removed or expired; stopping the node does not erase its database.

This one's a demo. The real switch lives in the desktop app, in the companion and under You → Connection.

next · the packet relay

Block a node id. Done.

Next comes a packet relay inside the app, for computers that can't reach each other directly: it passes along QUIC packets it can't read at all. Running one means you pass along traffic you can't see, so you may one day hear from someone who says a device behind your relay misbehaved. You'll act on it without reading anything:

1Find the node id

A credible complaint names the device: a long id like 7c1e0f9a…, plus when and what happened.

2Block it

Paste it under Relay → Blocked with a short note to yourself. Your relay stops passing packets to and from that device right away.

3That's all

No packet inspection, no records of past traffic: there aren't any. Unblock whenever you like.

home node

Your desktop looks after your phone.

Phones can't keep connections open in the background, and browsers can't take connections at all. So your desktop, or a hoi-node, keeps your communities in sync and serves your phone and browser whenever they wake up. It's yours, it's at home, and nobody else is involved. A browser on the same computer pairs with one click, a phone with one scan: it reaches your computer over your Wi-Fi with WebRTC, encrypted, with no certificate to buy and no server in between.

Pair a phone or browser Scan it with your phone's camera, or let the app open it in your browser. https://hoi.sh/app/#/pair?u=ws://127.0.0.1:7842&t=<pairing token>&n=<name>&w=<certificate fingerprint>@<address>
1On the desktop

The companion (or You → Connection) shows a QR code and an Open hoi in my browser button. hoi-node prints the same link when it starts.

2On the phone

Point the camera at the code and tap Connect. The link carries the node's addresses, its certificate's fingerprint and a pairing token, nothing else. Tap Link this phone and compare four digits with your computer to give it your key.

3That's it

The pill at the top now says via your computer's name. A wrong token is turned away at the door, and a new pairing link makes the old one stop working. Each device keeps a key of its own, blessed by yours; you can revoke any of them under You → Your devices.

hoi-node · build it today

A peer node with no website.

Anyone can run the same peer software on their own machine. Public mode accepts and provides peer connections without a website, user accounts or a personal-data sign-up form. The static client connects directly to it. It stores public content, so this is more than temporary packet forwarding. The repository includes a Dockerfile and self-hosting instructions; --peer-only disables the paired-device control listener.

Home node

Keeps your communities warm and serves your phone and browser.

Pinner

Holds a community's whole live window so newcomers can catch up from it. Moderators pin by default; anyone can. What pinners are responsible for.

Keeps them reachable

Members' browsers catch up from it over WebRTC on UDP 7844: on a VPS with a public address, no domain and no certificate needed. Same rules as the switch: no logs, a daily cap; --no-relay turns it off.

A public node

Every home node is one unless you start it with --no-public-relay: it carries open communities for anyone who asks, tells whoever connects about the other public nodes it reached, and prints a link anyone can open to find what it carries. Nobody runs a directory: a node you keep on is where people find communities.
