Keys & identity

A person on hoi is a key: an Ed25519 root key that did some memory-hard work once, written as a did:key. Each device signs with a key of its own, blessed by the root. This page covers how keys are made and kept, how devices are added, linked, revoked and burned, how a person comes back from a recovery line or from three friends without losing their standing, how names work, and how an age credential is checked.

Root keys

Every key in hoi is Ed25519 (ed25519-dalek): a 32-byte secret seed and a 32-byte public key. A person's root key is their identity: its public key is their id for good, in every community. There is no account, no email, no server that knows it.

People can paste a key as hex or as a did:key (parse_key). The example is the did:key specification's own Ed25519 example, which a unit test in handle.rs pins.

Weak keys

A public key must be a valid curve point outside the small-order subgroup (key_is_strong). Weak keys can make signatures that verify for many messages, so no identity, no root of a delegation, no device, no recovery key and no community drop key may be one. Signatures are checked with verify_strict.

Making a key

A new key comes from the system's randomness. The web app's first run also lets the person stir: pointer moves, taps and key presses are folded with their timing through SHA-256 (stir.ts), and the core mixes that digest, at most MAX_STIR (64) bytes, into fresh randomness of its own:

seed = BLAKE3-derive-key("hoi 2026 key seed v1", csprng[32] ‖ stir)

The system's 32 bytes alone make the key safe; the stir can only add to them, and the same gestures never make the same key. What is generated this way is not the root itself but the person's master secret: the root and every later root are derived from it (the recovery line). The device gets a key of its own, generated the same way.

The identity record

A root key becomes an identity by publishing an identity record: no community, no parent, signed by the root, expiring after KEEP (a hundred years).

worku64
The nonce of the root's memory-hard identity work: Argon2id(root ‖ u64be(work), salt "hoi/identity/v1\0", 8 MiB, 1 pass), counted in leading zero bits (Stamps & work).
rcbytes32, optional
A commitment to the recovery key that may replace this root.
ofbytes32, optional
For a root that replaces an earlier one: the person it continues.

Identities carry at least MIN_BITS (4); new ones are mined to DEFAULT_BITS (10), well above the communities' default ask of 8; a community may ask for up to MAX_BITS (20). More work can be added later from the device that holds the root (You → Key work in the web app, two bits a step): the stronger identity record replaces the weaker one, and must repeat the same rc and of, since a root that commits to two different things is burned. A device's network address is a separate key again (its iroh endpoint), so peers can't tie where a device is to what its person posts.

Device keys and delegations

The root key doesn't sign posts. Each device signs with its own device key, and a delegation record says which root it speaks for. Losing a phone then costs one device, not the identity.

The root key that blesses the device. Never the device itself.

The device's seal key: X25519, derived from the device key (BLAKE3-derive-key("hoi drop key seal v1", device secret), then HPKE's DeriveKeyPair) and used only for taking things sealed to this device: community drop keys and friends' recovery shares. Signed by the device, not the root.
/// What a root signs to bless a device: the BLAKE3 of
/// `canonical_cbor({t: "hoi/delegation/1", root, device, scope, expires_at})`.
pub fn delegation_statement(root: &Id, device: &Id, scope: &[String], expires_at: u64) -> Id {
    let v = Value::map([
        ("t", t("hoi/delegation/1")),
        ("root", b32(root)),
        ("device", b32(device)),
        ("scope", Value::A(scope.iter().map(|s| t(s)).collect())),
        ("expires_at", Value::U(expires_at)),
    ]);
    Id::hash(&v.encode())
}

The rules that keep identities apart

  • Two signatures. The record's signature by the device and root_sig by the root must both check, and both keys must be strong.
  • First root wins. A device belongs to the first root that blessed it. A delegation naming another root for a known device is refused ("that device already belongs to another identity"); the store keeps the existing blessing unless the same root renews it with a later expiry.
  • Devices aren't identities. A key with an identity record can't be blessed as a device, and a device key can't publish an identity. A key can't be its own device.
  • Sixteen at once. A root may have at most 16 live (unexpired, unrevoked) devices; a seventeenth needs a revocation first.
  • Strangers' keys. Delegations, like identities and revocations, travel with the records they make valid, and a device takes them from strangers only for people it has heard of (Validation pipeline).
  • Time. A device's record counts only if it was made no later than the blessing's expires_at and before any revocation.

Renewal

While a device holds the root key, it renews its own blessing when the node opens and fewer than 30 days are left, or when the blessing carries no seal key: a new delegation for the same device and root with a later expiry, which replaces the old one everywhere.

lanes: New device | Root device | Peers -- Request -- New device: a fresh device key, kept as pending New device -> Root device: hoi:link: {t: link-req, device, label} -- Blessing -- Root device: device isn't the root, isn't an identity Root device: root_sig = sign(root, delegation_statement(root, device, ["*"], now + 180 days)) Root device --> New device: hoi:link: {t: link-ok, root, person, chain, device, scope, created, expires, root_sig, identity, name, genesis} -- Consent -- New device: takes the identity and the person's chain in; the root must be the person's current root New device: delegation {root, scope, expires_at, root_sig, seal}, 16-bit stamp, signed with the device key New device => Peers @coral: recs [identity, delegation, …] with its records Peers: both signatures, strong keys, at most 400 days, first root wins, at most 16 live devices

Each record carries its carriers: when a device gossips one of its person's records, the author's identity and device blessing (and a replaced person's chain) go along, so a receiver can check it on its own. A person's own identity and blessings go first hand only to their own devices; everyone else gets them with the next record they come with.

The vector core/vectors/delegation-chain.json holds exactly this chain: the root's identity record (with its Argon2id work), the delegation signed by the device with root_sig over delegation_statement (the vector gives the statement's hash), and a post signed by the device key. A receiver must hold both of the first two to accept the third.

Linking a device

Two ways to bless a new device, both ending in the same approval: by hand with codes (above), or in one scan through the home node both devices pair with (link.rs). The web app's LinkDevice screen offers both: a QR code that pairs a phone with the same home node and asks to be linked, and the three codes (linkRequest, linkApprove, linkComplete) for when there's no home node or no camera.

The codes

Both codes are hoi:link: followed by base64url of a canonical CBOR map. The request names the new device's public key and a label (cleaned like a name). The approval carries everything the new device needs to start: the root and the person (different after a recovery), the person's chain of later roots and rotations, the blessing's scope, times and root_sig, the identity record, the person's name, and the geneses of their public communities, up to 8 of them in 64 KiB for codes typed or scanned by hand, up to 64 in 256 KiB through the home node. Private community geneses and MLS keys never go this way.

link_complete refuses an approval for another device, an identity record that isn't the person's, or a root that no longer speaks for the person ("That approval comes from a root that no longer speaks for this person."). Then the new device signs its own delegation, publishes it, and joins the communities the approval carried.

Through the home node

Every device paired with a home node gets the home node's link key in its hello (link_key, 32 bytes). link messages are {mailbox, sealed}: mailbox is the first 16 bytes of BLAKE3-derive-key("hoi 2026 link mailbox v1", link key), and sealed is XChaCha20-Poly1305 under BLAKE3-derive-key("hoi 2026 link box v1", link key). A home node passes a link message from one of its devices on to its other devices, unread. The home node holds the key too, so this hides nothing about linking from it; what gives the approval its authority is the device's signed consent and the root's blessing, as with codes.

To stop another device paired with the same home from passing its own request off as yours, the four digits both screens show are a commit-then-reveal comparison, like Bluetooth's numeric comparison: neither side can steer them.

/// Four digits from the device key and both sides' nonces.
pub(crate) fn check_code(device: &Id, theirs: &[u8; 32], ours: &[u8; 16]) -> String {
    let mut input = Vec::with_capacity(80);
    input.extend_from_slice(&device.0);
    input.extend_from_slice(theirs);
    input.extend_from_slice(ours);
    let h = blake3::derive_key(CHECK_CONTEXT, &input);
    format!("{:04}", u32::from_le_bytes(h[..4].try_into().expect("4 bytes")) % 10_000)

Browsers that keep no key

A browser can link with the remote flag. Then the device with the root makes the browser's device key itself, blesses it, signs its consent and keeps it; the browser holds only the public key and the delegation. It builds and stamps its records as before and asks over the home link for each signature (sign {device, rec}, answered signed {device, id, sig} or unsigned {device, id, reason}). Posts, comments, votes and the like are signed without asking; ledger entries wait for a yes on the computer. Turning the computer off turns its browsers' signing off; revoking the device there ends it for good.

Revoking a device

From the device that holds the root (You → Your devices, revoke_device), a revoke record { device } signed by the root, with no community, kept a hundred years. Only a root signs one, and the app won't revoke the device it is running on ("You can't revoke the device you're using.").

  • Records the device made at or after the revocation's created_at are refused everywhere (Revoked). What it signed before stays valid.
  • Only a root revokes, and only the root that blessed the device. A revocation that arrives before the delegation it revokes is remembered by (device, root) and applies when that root's blessing turns up, so a stranger's revoke for a device it never blessed can't hide the real root's.
  • Of several revocations, the earliest counts.
  • A keyless browser this computer signed for loses its key here at once.

Burning an identity

For a root key that leaked: a revoke whose device is the root itself (burn_identity, from the device holding the root, confirmed by typing the key's short form). Every device that takes it in drops everything the person ever signed, and refuses anything they sign later (Burned); the burn record itself stays, so the burn keeps spreading. There is no undo, and no way to carry standing over to a new key.

A root is also burned when it signs two identity records that commit to different things (a different rc or of): someone who took the root and tries to commit it to their own recovery key burns it instead, and then nobody can replace it. Burning a root that speaks for a person burns the person.

Burning versus recovery

Burning ends a person. Recovery, below, keeps the person and replaces the root under them: use it when the recovery line is still yours.

The recovery line

The person's master secret, written as one line for a password manager or a piece of paper:

hoi-key-1-xxxxxxxx-xxxxxxxx-xxxxxxxx-xxxxxxxx-xxxxxxxx-xxxxxxxx-xxxxxxxx
secret | 32 B | coral | 8 | the master secret check | 3 B | sun | 2 | first 3 bytes of BLAKE3-derive-key("hoi 2026 recovery key v1", secret)

The groups are z-base-32, which has no 0, l, v or 2, so the line survives being read aloud or written down. Decoding forgives what people and password managers do to it: any letter case, missing or extra hyphens, spaces and line breaks, 0 for o and l for 1. The check catches a typo and refuses a stranger's string, and says nothing about the key, being a keyed hash of the secret itself. A line that doesn't start with the prefix is "That isn't a hoi recovery key. It starts with hoi-key-1-."; a damaged one asks to check it against the password manager.

The line is the key

It isn't an encrypted copy: whoever reads it can be you. It belongs in a password manager, which is encrypted, or in a drawer. The web app's backup screen offers to save it with the browser's password manager (login "hoi key · name · short key") and never shows it unless asked.

Generations

Every root the person will ever sign with, and every recovery key that can replace one, is derived from the master secret:

KeyDerivationRole
root_at(master, g)BLAKE3-derive-key("hoi 2026 root key v1", master ‖ u32le(g)) as an Ed25519 seedgeneration 0 is the person, for good; later ones replace it
recovery_at(master, g)BLAKE3-derive-key("hoi 2026 recovery key v1 by generation", master ‖ u32le(g))may replace the root of generation g − 1 with the root of g
commitment(key)BLAKE3-derive-key("hoi 2026 recovery commitment v1", key)what an identity record carries as rc, so the recovery key is shown only when used

The identity record of the root of generation g commits to recovery_at(master, g + 1) and, for g > 0, names the person in of. A chain has at most 64 generations.

Forgetting the line

A device keeps the master secret only until the person says they saved the line ("I saved it. Forget it here", forget_recovery_key), or while it restores. From then on a stolen device gives a thief the root of the day but never the power to replace it. A device that no longer keeps the line says so, and can't give shares to friends.

Replacing a root, keeping the standing

Restoring from the line always replaces the root (restore_identity). The new device:

  1. Starts as the person

    Decodes the line, derives the person (root_at(master, 0)), makes a fresh device key, and marks itself restoring. If it has never seen the person's first identity record it makes it again, with the minimum work and the same commitment, so it agrees with the original wherever both are.

  2. Finds the current root

    The last root of the person's chain, as far as it knows, and its generation g (searching up to 64).

  3. Makes the next root

    The root of g + 1 publishes its identity (minimum work, committing to recovery_at(g + 2), naming the person in of).

  4. Rotates

    A rotate { person, to } record signed by recovery_at(g + 1): the recovery key the current root committed to.

  5. Blesses itself

    The new root blesses this device.

  6. Catches up

    If it later learns the chain had gone further, it rotates again from there; every root and key is derived, so nothing else is needed. It stops restoring once a peer has told it about the person (their identity or a record of theirs came from the network).

lanes: New device | Peers | Old device -- Restore -- New device: line → master; person = root_at(master, 0); fresh device key New device: current root = root g; derives root g + 1 and recovery key g + 1 New device => Peers @coral: identity of root g + 1 {work, rc, of: person} New device => Peers @coral: rotate {person, to: root g + 1}, signed by recovery key g + 1 New device => Peers @coral: delegation of the new device by root g + 1 -- Everywhere -- Peers: the rotation's checks; the person now signs with root g + 1 Peers: records of root g and its devices made from the rotation's time on are refused Peers -> Old device: the rotation Old device: replaced: nothing it signs counts any more Peers --> New device: the person's records; restoring ends

What a rotation must satisfy

  1. A strong recovery key

    The rotate record's author is a valid, strong key.

  2. A first identity

    The person has an identity record, and it continues no one.

  3. The committed key

    The author's commitment equals the rc of one of the person's roots ("not the recovery key this identity committed to" otherwise).

  4. A root that says whom it continues

    to has an identity record whose of names the person.

  5. A root nobody else has

    to isn't a device, isn't already in the chain, and doesn't speak for someone else.

  6. In order

    The root being replaced took over before this rotation was made, and wasn't already replaced by then.

The earliest valid rotation away from each root counts, by (created_at, id). The store then maps every later root to the person (person_of), so records signed by the new root and its devices count for the person: their ladder standing, vouches, work (the best identity along the chain) and history. The replaced root and its devices sign nothing that counts from the rotation's time on; what they signed before stays. A new root that continues someone is no one until its rotation arrives, and its records come again with it. Records that travel for a replaced person carry the chain (each later root's identity and each rotation) so others can follow it; the .hoikey file and device link approvals carry it too.

core/vectors/records.json includes a rotation: identity-committed (a first root whose rc is the commitment to the recovery key), identity-continues (the new root, naming the person in of) and rotate (signed by that recovery key, from the person to the new root).

Recovery through friends

The master secret can also be split among friends, any three of whom bring it back, none of whom can read it alone. Nothing passes through a server.

A key split into five pieces, one per friend. Pick any three and the key comes back; with two, the result is noise.

Shamir's secret sharing: any three of five pieces rebuild the secret; two say nothing about it.

The split

shamir.rs is Shamir's secret sharing over GF(2⁸), byte by byte, in the AES field (x⁸ + x⁴ + x³ + x + 1). Each byte of the 32-byte secret is the constant term of a random polynomial of degree k − 1; share x (1–255) holds the polynomials' values at x. Since it runs on secrets, multiplication and inversion (a²⁵⁴) take the same steps whatever the bytes: no tables, no branches on secret values. combine interpolates at 0 and refuses repeated points, unequal lengths and fewer than two shares; with fewer shares than the threshold the answer is noise, which the person check below catches.

x | 1 B | sun | 1 | the share's point, 1–255 y | 32 B | lilac | 8 | the 32 polynomials at x

The vector core/vectors/shamir.json pins a fixed secret (bytes 0x10–0x2f), n = 5, k = 3, the 64 coefficient bytes (c₁ first, for each byte in turn), the five shares as x ‖ y, and the secret again from shares 2–4. The web app's first-run game rehearses the same 3-of-5 split on the person's public key, real arithmetic with nothing secret in it.

Giving shares

From a device that still keeps the line, in a community the person is in that isn't private (give_shares; the web app's Recovery friends screen): pick three to seven people other than yourself. Each must have at least one device whose blessing carries a seal key ("… has no device that can keep a share yet. Ask them to open hoi once."); up to 16 devices each are used.

  1. Split

    shamir::split(master, n, 3), n the number of friends.

  2. Seal

    Each friend's 33-byte share is sealed to each of their devices' seal keys with HPKE (base mode, DHKEM(X25519, HKDF-SHA256), HKDF-SHA256, ChaCha20-Poly1305), info hoi recovery share v1, bound to "hoi/recovery-share/1" ‖ person ‖ friend ‖ device: 32 bytes of encapsulated key, 33 of share, 16 of tag, 81 bytes in all.

  3. Publish

    One share record per friend in that community: parent is the friend, sealed a list of {d: device, s: 81 bytes}, one per device, and seq the time; kept a hundred years. The latest seq per (person, friend) counts. Friends no longer named get an empty sealed: theirs is taken back.

lanes: Your device | Community | Friend's devices Your device: needs the line here; 3 to 7 friends with seal keys Your device: split the master 3 of n; seal share i to each device of friend i (81 bytes each) Your device => Community @lilac: share {sealed: [{d, s}…], seq}, parent = friend, one per friend Community => Friend's devices @lilac: synced like any record of the community Friend's devices: list you among the people whose share they keep

Asking for them back

On a new device without a key, ask_friends makes a one-time X25519 key and shows a code, hoi-ask-1- and base64url of its public half, and six digits, BLAKE3-derive-key("hoi recovery request digits v1", key) read as a little-endian u32 mod 1,000,000 (123 456). The person sends the code to friends however they like.

lanes: New device | Friend | Friend's device New device: ask_friends: one-time key, code hoi-ask-1-…, six digits New device -> Friend: the code, by any channel Friend -> Friend's device: pastes the code Friend's device --> Friend: the same six digits New device .. Friend: the person reads the digits out; the friend compares Friend's device: opens the share sealed to it; seals (person ‖ share) to the request's key Friend's device --> Friend: answer hoi-share-1-… Friend -> New device: the answer, by any channel New device: three answers for one person: combine; root_at(master, 0) must be the person New device: restores as from the line, a rotation included

The answer is person (32) ‖ share (33), sealed with the same HPKE suite to the request's key, info hoi recovery answer v1, bound to the request key, so it opens for no other request. take_answer keeps one answer per (person, point); at three it combines them and checks that generation 0 of the result is the person ("Those answers don't fit together: one of them is from another request, or a share is wrong."). Then the device restores exactly as from the line: a new root, a rotation, the standing kept.

The key file

The .hoikey file is the passphrase-protected backup, for a disk or a cloud (export_identity). Its passphrase must be at least 15 characters (at most 1,024 bytes). It is sealed with keystore.rs:

HOIK | 4 B | sun | 2 | magic 1 | 1 B | sun | 1 | version m_cost | 4 B | sky | 2 | Argon2id memory in KiB, u32 LE t_cost | 4 B | sky | 2 | passes, u32 LE p_cost | 1 B | sky | 1 | lanes salt | 16 B | lilac | 4 | random nonce | 24 B | lilac | 6 | random, XChaCha20 ciphertext | n B | coral | 10 | the plaintext, XChaCha20-Poly1305 tag | 16 B | mute | 2 | Poly1305

The key is Argon2id(passphrase, salt, m_cost, t_cost, p_cost), 32 bytes. The 54-byte header is the AEAD's associated data, so nobody can swap the parameters. Files are written at 64 MiB and 3 passes, one lane (about a second on a laptop, a few in a browser). A file sets its own cost, so open caps it before any work: 8 to 256 MiB, 1 to 16 passes, 1 to 8 lanes, or it is refused as an unsupported format. A wrong passphrase and a damaged file look the same: "wrong passphrase or damaged file".

The local vault and passkeys

On a device, keys and community data live in the local vault (vault.rs): one random 32-byte data key, wrapped once per way in, and storage cells sealed with it. Encryption protects a locked database, not an unlocked process, nor rollback to a complete older copy of the database. The storage layers are on Storage.

A new vault's passphrase must be at least 15 characters (at most 1,024 bytes). Unlocking accepts only a passphrase slot of exactly 102 bytes at the one supported cost (64 MiB, 3 passes, 1 lane), so a planted header can't make the device run an expensive key derivation. A cell is sealed with BLAKE3-derive-key("hoi local vault cell v1:" + address, data key) and XChaCha20-Poly1305, the context doubling as associated data: a cell moved to another address doesn't open.

Passkeys in the web app

A passkey can open the browser's vault as the passphrase does (You → Unlock with a passkey; not offered in the desktop shell). It uses WebAuthn's prf extension: the authenticator answers 32 bytes for a salt the vault keeps in the clear, so the page can ask before anything is unlocked.

  1. Make

    makePasskey (passkey.ts) creates a credential for relying party hoi with a random user id, named hoi · name · platform, algorithms ES256 (−7) and RS256 (−257), resident key preferred, user verification required, and asks it to evaluate the PRF on a fresh 32-byte salt. Authenticators that don't answer at creation are asked once more right away.

  2. Wrap

    The worker wraps the vault's data key with BLAKE3-derive-key("hoi local vault passkey wrap v1", prf ‖ salt), sealed under the context hoi local vault passkey slot v1, in a new slot. The same credential replaces its own slot; a fifth passkey is refused.

  3. Unlock

    The unlock page lists the slots' credential ids and salts, asks the authenticator (user verification required) with each credential's own salt, and hands the worker only the credential id and the 32-byte answer.

A passkey only opens this browser's vault. The passphrase stays, and the recovery line stays the way back to the person. The passkey itself never signs anything on hoi.

Names

Keys are the identity; names are what people read. Three kinds, and none of them is unique or global:

NameWho sets itWhere it lives
Display namethe person, for themselvesthe name field of their posts and comments; each device keeps the newest it saw per person
Petnameyou, for someone else (set_petname)this device only, and your key file; never sent
Device labelyou, for one of your devicesthis device only; never sent

A device shows a person by the petname you gave them, else their newest display name, else their short key. Every name passes the name rules (at most 32 characters, no leading, trailing or doubled spaces, nothing that looks like a key, no direction marks or invisible characters: Text, links & names). Anyone can claim any display name, so the app shows the key alongside.

Names that are domains

A person whose display name is a domain they control (mira.example.org) can prove it, and devices that see the name check for themselves:

ProofSaysChecked by
a DNS TXT record on _hoi.<domain>did=did:key:z…native hosts: the system's own resolvers (up to three from /etc/resolv.conf, no fixed provider), UDP port 53, 3 seconds each, up to 16 TXT strings
a file at https://<domain>/.well-known/hoia line did:key:z…native hosts by the rules of link previews; browsers with fetch (no credentials, no referrer, no cache, 5 seconds), which only works when the domain sends Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *; the first 4,096 bytes, 8 lines

A name counts as a domain by as_domain (two labels or more, a letter top-level label or punycode; Text, links & names). The core asks its host for a check when a post or comment brings such a name and on each sweep, but a domain is checked again only after a day (FRESH), a failed one after an hour (RETRY), at most 60 checks an hour whatever arrives, a check that never came back given up after 60 seconds, and at most 512 domains remembered. With "Only through my VPN", the DNS question leaves from the VPN's address, and nothing leaves while the VPN is down.

The answer: ✓ verified when the domain's answers name this person's key exactly, "not verified" when they don't or nothing answered, and "checking" until then. A name that merely looks like a domain gets no badge. Nothing about the check is published: each device decides for itself.

Community names in registries are a different thing: names for community keys, kept by registries (Registry).

Profile pictures and colors

A person's picture is a JPEG of at most 4,096 bytes, square, at most 128 pixels a side, signed into a profile record per community; an empty one restores the identicon drawn from the key. A profile-color record picks the identicon's palette: mint, sky, lilac, peach, sun, or empty for the key's own. Both are kept on the device with a revision and published into a community after the person's next post or comment there; pictures only once the person is trusted there (or an owner or moderator), colors always. The newest per person and community counts. The details, from the web app's encoder to how devices decide whose pictures to show, are on Media.

Age credentials

A community whose admission is age-proof lets a newcomer in once a moderator saw a proof that they are over 18 and nothing else (age.rs; the web app's AgeProof). The proof is an SD-JWT VC presentation from an identity wallet. The community's limits name the issuer keys it trusts (age_issuers; an age-proof community must name at least one).

  1. The challenge

    age_challenge shows what the wallet binds its presentation to: aud = hoi:<community hex>, nonce = base64url of the first 16 bytes of BLAKE3-derive-key("hoi age proof nonce v1", person ‖ community), and the trusted issuers.

  2. The newcomer's device checks

    age_proof_code runs the checks below and makes a code: hoi-age-1- and base64url of person ‖ community ‖ presentation. The newcomer sends it to a moderator however they like.

  3. The moderator's device checks again

    admit_by_age: the community must ask for age proofs and the person not be in yet; the same checks; then an admit ledger entry whose reason is just "age proof". The presentation is never published.

#CheckRefused with
1At most 16 KiB; issuer-JWT~disclosure~…~KB-JWT with a key binding at the endthe presentation has no key binding: it could be anyone's
2The issuer JWT is signed by one of the trusted keys: ES256 with a P-256 key (SEC1, 33 or 65 bytes) or EdDSA with an Ed25519 key (32 bytes)not signed by an issuer this community trusts
3exp not passed; _sd_alg, if present, is sha-256the credential has expired · a digest other than SHA-256
4Every disclosure's SHA-256 (base64url) is listed in _sd, and every disclosure is [salt, "age_over_18", value]a disclosure the issuer didn't sign · only age_over_18 may be disclosed
5age_over_18 is true, disclosed or plainthe credential doesn't say over 18
6The KB-JWT is signed by the holder key in cnf.jwk (EC P-256 or OKP Ed25519), with typ kb+jwtthe key binding isn't one
7Its aud and nonce are this community's and this person'sthe proof was made for someone else, or another community
8Its iat is within the last 15 minutes and at most 60 seconds aheadthe proof is too old; make a new one
9Its sd_hash is the SHA-256 of the presentation up to its last ~the key binding is for another presentation

Until a moderator admits them, the newcomer's posts in an age-proof community are stored and held: "this community lets people in once a moderator saw a proof of their age" (Validation pipeline). How labelers and trust interact with age proofs is on Trust & ranking.
